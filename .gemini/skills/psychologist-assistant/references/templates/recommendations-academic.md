
# Recommendations and identification of academic and behavioral needs to access the general curriculum: Academic

## Academic / Phonological Processing

- To help _firstname improve their ability to use phoneme-grapheme relationships, discourage reliance on pictures as aids for word recognition.
- Praise _firstname for any attempts they makes at pronouncing unknown words when reading aloud. Encourage them to try and identify words rather than guessing or skipping over words.
- Provide explicit instruction in phonic elements. The focus of phonics instruction should be to help _firstname see how letters relate to sounds, and then how letters and sounds are used to read and spell words.
- Use the words in _firstname's sight vocabulary as a basis for building phonic skills. For example, start with a word that they automatically recognizes, such as run, and then show them how that pattern can help them identify a new word, such as fun. Help promote generalization of common patterns by frequently pointing out similarities in words.
- Prior to having _firstname read a passage, underline any multisyllabic words that they may have difficulty pronouncing. Review pronunciation of the words. Have _firstname then practice reading the words fluently in context.

## Academic / Decoding

- Use a multi-sensory approach to help _firstname learn about each letter of the alphabet. For instance, have them write letters in shaving cream, color all the letter A's they sees in a book, or use letter magnets to play different games together. The goal is to increase letter knowledge first then move on to letter sounds and eventually letters within words.
- Teach _firstname how to chunk words. Chunking is breaking down words into smaller parts. Ask them to look at a word and break up each letter and sound out each letter within the word. After _firstname masters breaking down individual letters, increase to 2 letter chunks.

## Academic / Letter Identification

- Teach _firstname to sing the alphabet song. Prior familiarity with the letter names will make it easier for them to learn the association between the names and the letter shapes. When teaching the song, however, make sure that _firstname knows where the breaks are between letter names. For example, they needs to be thinking, 'L, M, N, O, P' rather than 'elemenopee'.
- If _firstname has difficulty learning some of the letter names, make drawings out of those letters to strengthen the association between shape and name. For example, make a bee out of an uppercase B.
- Provide multisensory instruction to aid in letter learning. When teaching _firstname to associate letters with their names, teach only one letter-name at a time. To promote retention and attention, incorporate tactile-kinesthetic activities into instruction. Activities may include having _firstname trace glitter letters while saying the letter name, put letter forms into their proper place in a puzzle jig while reminding themself of its name, or, working with another student, making the shape of the letter with their bodies while saying (shouting, singing) its name.
- Guide _firstname to activate, organize, and develop knowledge of the relationship between letters and words. Games are particularly helpful in this regard. For example, play games requiring students to come up with words starting with specified letters, such as Grandmother's Trip (go through the alphabet but do not require _firstname to recall a previous items named) or games wherein _firstname has to name objects within a given category and starting with a particular letter (e.g., 'My family owns a pet store and they sell something that begins with the letter C'. With _firstname's help, brainstorm as many words as you can generate. Continue the game with another letter).

## Academic / Reading Fluency

- Use guided repeated oral reading to improve word recognition and fluency. Have _firstname read the same passage several times orally. Provide them with systematic and explicit feedback and guidance.
- Have _firstname listen to a recorded (audio-book) passage or a short book several times as they reads along with the audio-book. When they has mastered the passage or book, have them read it to someone else.
- Because _firstname's decoding is slow and effortful, most of _firstname attention is directed to word read accuracy rather than trying to understand what they are reading. Be sure to provide _firstname with necessary support in the classroom. This may include taped books, peer readers, extended time, and modified instructional materials.
- Teach _firstname how to slow down their reading when they encounters difficult material and to reread passages when the meaning is unclear.

## Academic / Reading Comprehension

- When providing instruction in reading comprehension, ensure that _firstname's instructional materials are at the independent reading level in word recognition. Materials at the independent level will allow _firstname to devote their full attention to the comprehension activities.
- Present meaningful reading assignments to motivate _firstname's intent to understand the reading material. For example, within text have _firstname locate and take notes on information that they can discuss with a small group.
- When choosing reading comprehension materials for _firstname, evaluate the books on decoding level and sentence cohesiveness. Be aware that high-interest, low-vocabulary books may obstruct comprehension rather than facilitate it.
- Schedule time daily for reading authentic texts. Read texts to _firstname that will help increase their knowledge base and vocabulary, and encourage them to read a variety of different types of text.
- Provide _firstname with daily readings of text that are at the independent or instructional reading levels. When text at the frustration level is unavoidable (e.g., entire class reading an on-grade level book), provide supported reading (e.g., partner reading, taped books).
- Before _firstname reads a textbook, color-code with a yellow highlighter the sections that are most important to read.

## Academic / Reading Vocabulary

- Make flashcards with words on the front and definitions on the back for words that _firstname does not know. Mix in word cards that they does now to build up confidence for the task.
- Use words in place of known words in the same sentence to help build vocabulary. For instance, you could say 'I am so cold, it is frigid in here'.
- Encourage _firstname to ask questions about words that are unfamiliar to them.

## Academic / Math Concepts and Application

- Immediately after _firstname has demonstrated what appears to be true understanding of a process or concept, ask them if they would be willing to teach it to someone else. Teaching forces one to clarify their own understanding of a process. Make sure the teacher is available to help if they needs help.
- Teach _firstname how to use a number line, counting forward and backward, to solve addition and subtraction problems.
- When reviewing computations with _firstname, place the emphasis on how they solved the problems, rather than on the accuracy of the solutions.
- Help _firstname develop self-monitoring skills by estimating answers to math calculations. Have them estimate the answer and write it by the side of a problem before they calculates the answer.
- Before _firstname works a page of math computation problems, have them color code the operation signs. Help them decide on a color for each of the four signs and consistently highlight or trace each in its own color.

## Academic / Computation

- Continuously reinforce previously learned facts interspersed with practice of more recently learned facts. Continue fact practice even when _firstname seems to know them automatically.
- Do not have _firstname compete with other students when practicing math facts. Instead, have _firstname keep their own private performance chart. Draw an analogy to a runner's 'Personal Best.'
- At this time, do not spend time trying to get _firstname to learn facts that they has historically been unable to recall. Instead, for computation problems, give _firstname pocket-sized charts with addition and multiplication facts. Teach _firstname how to use the addition chart for subtraction and the multiplication chart for division. _firstname may use the chart any time _firstname wants for the answers to math facts but encourage _firstname to guess the answer before looking. That way, _firstname gets immediate reinforcement if _firstname is right and immediate correction if _firstname is wrong. Additionally, eventually, visualizing the location of the answer may help in retrieving it. When a fact has become truly automatic, _firstname should black it out. This will continuously strengthen their ability to recall the fact as well as allow _firstname to see how many facts _firstname has left to learn.
- Have practice sessions with flash cards so _firstname can enhance their math fluency. Additionally, using flash cards that have the answer recorded on the reverse side, have _firstname practice facts for several minutes daily. Provide frequent review of the newly mastered facts.

## Academic / Written Expression

- Before writing, have _firstname consider the purpose, their point of view, and the audience who will be reading their paper.
- Adapt all writing assignments to match _firstname's present level of oral language skill. Adjust the grading criteria accordingly.
- Reduce the amount of writing that _firstname is expected to complete, both in the classroom and for homework.
- Provide daily guided discussions that will help _firstname increase their awareness of the strategies that _firstname uses when writing. Use brief teacher-student conferences to help _firstname understand how _firstname approaches writing tasks and then how they can use alternative strategies.

## Academic / Spelling

- Integrate instruction in spelling and word identification so that _firstname sees the connections between phonemes and graphemes as well as between graphemes and phonemes.
- Emphasize the functional use of spelling. Demonstrate the importance of correct spelling in practical and social situations.
- Provide _firstname with a reduced list of spelling words. Begin with five words a week. As performance improves, increase the number of words on the list.
- Dictate to _firstname words with phonically regular spellings, one at a time. Pronounce the sounds of the word separately and slowly in sequence. Remind them to write each letter sound as they hears it.
- Provide corrective feedback to _firstname immediately after completion of a spelling test. Have _firstname touch each letter of the word with their pencil as you spell the word out loud. Have them correct each word that has an error.

## Academic / Listening Comprehension

- Use Differentiated Instruction to teach lessons in a variety of ways.
- Use repetition frequently and ask _firstname to repeat information back in their own words.
- Provide copies of notes so _firstname can refer to their notes during lessons.

## Academic / Oral Expression

- Allow ample opportunities to practice without penalty (e.g., brainstorming, conferencing, sharing).
- Provide questions/topics in advance to allow time for preparation.
- Increase the 'wait time' for expecting a response. For instance, count silently to 10.
- Incorporate oral recitation activities such as poetry readings, parts in plays, etc. to help _firstname build expressive fluency.
- Let _firstname speak regularly through combinations of the following: a) answering questions and participating in discussions, b) taking positions and arguing those positions in class, c) debating others, d) making formal and informal presentations, and e) engaging in or analyzing oral expression processes.
