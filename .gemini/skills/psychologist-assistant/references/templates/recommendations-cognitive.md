
# Recommendations and identification of academic and behavioral needs to access the general curriculum: Cognitive

## Cognitive / Verbal Comprehension

- Pair oral instruction with demonstrations, visual examples, and models. Use concrete references familiar to _firstname when presenting new vocabulary or concepts.
- Word instructions clearly, using specific language and vocabulary that is familiar to _firstname. Avoid long and complex sentences.
- During instruction stick to the point - avoid digressions and excessive language.
- Insure understanding for present information before introducing additional information.
- Assist _firstname to express ideas by providing necessary vocabulary words.
- A lack of information and vocabulary may affect reading comprehension. As a result, orally review information _firstname read about in a passage. When necessary, reinterpret information using familiar vocabulary, terms and experiences.
- Use direct instruction methods to teach new words. Model use of the word, cite concrete references to enhance understanding of the word, have _firstname use the word in various tasks and contexts, and provide immediate feedback/error correction. Attempt to link new vocabulary to prior learning and familiar experiences.
- Attempt to expand _firstname's vocabulary by substituting more sophisticated or precise terms for them. For instance, when _firstname says their dog 'runs around and barks', restate the description by stating, 'Oh, your dog is frisky.'

## Cognitive / Fluid Reasoning

- Due to difficulty with deductive reasoning, _firstname experiences problems using rules or a set of procedures to solve problems. As a result, use discovery learning to provide experiences to assist them in understanding why a rule or principle works. Work from the bottom up by showing them how observations result in the formation of a rule. After _firstname understands why, they might be able to generalize the use of a rule or procedure to solve new problems.
- Due to difficulty with inferential reasoning, _firstname may experience confusion with discovery learning in which they are expected to arrive at a rule to explain examples. _firstname appears to work best when a rule is stated or a well-defined set of steps is established to solve a problem.
- To promote understanding and generalization in use of a rule or procedure, clearly describe the rule or procedure and provide numerous concrete examples, particularly from _firstname's experience.
- Develop understanding of abstract concepts by describing concrete, familiar elements of the concept (e.g., fruit can be eaten, grows on trees, has a seed or stone and has skin). In addition, employ concrete examples including pictures, demonstrations and manipulatives.
- Break complex tasks or procedures into component parts. After _firstname masters one part, move onto the next.
- Help _firstname sort out relevant from irrelevant information when solving a problem.
- Move slowly when presenting new information and tie new concepts into previously mastered concepts and information.
- Teach new information in groups or families and clarify how the items or examples are alike.
- Provide a routine or practiced sequence for approaching a difficult or complex task. A practiced routine will help _firstname solve problems despite difficulty understanding how and why a procedure works. It may be necessary to define when and under what circumstances a procedure is to be used.

## Cognitive / Working Memory

- Use a top down approach to instruction in order to lessen memory demands. In this approach, the product is presented as a whole before its component parts are described or taught. For example, when teaching phonetic decoding, say the word first and then help _firstname phonetically analyze the word.
- Break instructions into parts, presenting only one or two parts at a time. Establish understanding or mastery of one component before moving onto the next.
- Teach information in easily recognized groups or families to facilitate memory.
- When teaching, provide a written or pictorial model that _firstname can refer to. Use of graphic organizers can be helpful. Write key terms on the board so they can readily refer to them.
- Repeat important information at a high rate, emphasizing key words by tone of voice.

## Cognitive / Long-Term Retrieval

- Employ multiple modalities (e.g., writing, speaking, listening, etc.) when _firstname rehearses rote information.
- Help _firstname learn to break a long list of information into small parts or chunks of seven or fewer items. Encourage _firstname to master one small group before moving to the next.
- Discuss the meaning of a story or text immediately after it is read. Ask _firstname to elaborate on what was read. Memory for information will be improved if _firstname has a good understanding of the concept and is able to make more associations.
- Provide opportunities for _firstname to use a concept or skill to solve a problem. _firstname is more likely to retain information which has been put into practice.
- Prompt _firstname to elaborate on and reason with information and concepts taught. For instance, they are more likely to remember that Paraguay is one of the poorest countries in South America if they develops reasons for this condition (e.g., land locked, small country, lack of natural resources, arid climate, etc.).
- Repetition: _firstname rehearses information by orally repeating it or writing it several times. Rehearsal will be most beneficial if it occurs shortly after learning and occurs intermittently thereafter.
- Acronym: _firstname forms a memorable (i.e., silly, outrageous, familiar) phrase or sentence using the first letters of the words or terms to be recalled (e.g., My waffle is under the new car to recall the seven mountain states of Montana, Wyoming, etc.).
- Method of Loci: This strategy involves _firstname imagining themself walking through a familiar location such as a bedroom or walking to school. Then, _firstname imagines placing a term, word or element to be recalled in prominent locations along the route. When it is necessary to recall the information, they mentally retraces the route. The mental image of the prominent locations along the route elicits the corresponding element to be recalled.

## Cognitive / Visual Processing

- Avoid relying excessively on visual models, diagrams and demonstrations during instruction with _firstname. Accompany visual demonstrations with oral explanations.
- When using visual demonstrations or models to teach a skill or concept, be prepared to move slowly and repeat visual demonstrations as needed.
- Break spatial tasks into component parts and provide a verbal set of instructions to match each part. For instance, provide a sequenced verbal strategy to help a student work through the steps of a math computation problem such as regrouping in subtraction.
- _firstname may experience difficulty forming a visual representation of a concept in their mind (e.g., change of solar position with latitude). As a result, provide hands on, concrete experiences and manipulatives when teaching an abstract concept that is visual in nature. Accompany these concrete experiences with verbal explanations.

## Cognitive / Auditory Processing

- When practical, word instructions individually to the student in order to avoid competition with extraneous background sounds.
- Seat _firstname in close proximity to the teacher in order to facilitate monitoring and to individualize instructions.
- Face _firstname when speaking.
- Articulate clearly and avoid speaking quickly.
- Word directions and instructions clearly, succinctly and in simple terms.
- Encourage _firstname to ask questions and seek help if they does not understand.

## Cognitive / Processing Speed

- Reduce the need for handwriting; provide _firstname with outlines of class notes.
- Shorten assignments when possible. For instance, have _firstname complete only even numbered problems on a worksheet.
- Provide extended time for tests and class assignments.
- Break down big projects and assignments into smaller ones with multiple deadlines instead of expecting one large project by one due date.
- Teach _firstname to cover their work so that only one or a few problems are visible; thus helping reduce distractions.
- Grade _firstname's work based on mastery instead of work completion.

## Cognitive / Visual Spatial

- During basic math computation practice, _firstname would benefit from being provided with a number line so they can refer back to it during math exercises.
- To support early reading skills, work with _firstname on understanding the sequence of letters in words. Give them a sheet of paper with a box for each letter within a word on it. Then provide them a cut out of each letter and ask them to create words. For instance, if the word is CAT give them a sheet of paper with three boxes and the letters C-A-T on separate pieces of paper. Ask them to place the letters in the boxes to spell CAT. This type of intervention reduces the load on their visual spatial sketchpad while still teaching the necessary skills for reading.

## Cognitive / Orthographic Processing

- To help with alphabetic knowledge, give _firstname a piece of paper or white board divided into four parts. Follow this sequence for each letter: (section one) trace the letter, (section 2) copy the letter, (section 3) write the letter from memory, (section 4) write with eyes closed.
- To help develop orthographical awareness, provide _firstname with extra practice reading/spelling high frequency irregular words (e.g. once, said) from word lists emphasizing (highlighting in some way) the irregular elements. (e.g. the ëaií grapheme in said).
- To help _firstname develop fluency, ensure daily reading of text that is at their level and practice and monitor progress of oral reading with repeated readings of continuous text.

## Cognitive / Automaticity

- To build automaticity, have _firstname participate in repeated practice of foundational concepts, such as identifying letters and knowing basic math facts.
- Even after _firstname seems to understand a concept and displays mastery, continue practicing basic skills to make them more automatic. This process is called overlearning and the more overlearned a subject is the more automatic it becomes.

## Cognitive / Cognitive Proficiency

- Provide _firstname with clear, consistent, and predictable expectations for behavior.
- Give advance notice of transitions, upcoming activities, etc.
- Allow more time responding to questions and decision making.
- Provide fewer choices.
- Break up larger tasks into smaller ones.
